How do we buy it without a failed tender?

Many tenders fail because of decisions made before the tender opens. These include how the lots are divided, how bids will be scored, and how much time bidders get.

  • Last reviewed 11 October 2026
  • 7 rules
  • About 5 min to read
  • Checklist for this guide

The 7 rules in this guide

  1. 3.1 Size each lot so that several suppliers can bid
  2. 3.2 Choose national or international competition deliberately
  3. 3.3 Tell bidders how bids will be scored
  4. 3.4 Give bidders enough time and clear answers
  5. 3.5 Learn from bidders' questions

3.1 Size each lot so that several suppliers can bid

A lot is a part of a tender that is bid for and awarded separately. You can divide lots by type of device, by region, or both. Their size decides who can bid. A lot that is too large keeps out capable smaller suppliers. Lots that are too small mean many contracts and can raise prices.

Example Instead of one national lot of 200 infant warmers, five regional lots of about 40 also let regional distributors bid.

Ask yourself For each lot, can you name several suppliers who could deliver the full quantity to every site?

3.2 Choose national or international competition deliberately

A national tender suits devices that Indonesian suppliers can make or supply in the quantity needed. An international tender brings in more bidders when they cannot. The choice affects the language, currency, timetable and delivery terms of the whole tender.

Example Before you decide, search Info Alkes for devices of this type with an AKD number, which means they are made in Indonesia.

Ask yourself Do you know who makes this device, and where, before you decide who can bid?

3.3 Tell bidders how bids will be scored

Bidders need to know the rules before they bid. Either the contract goes to the lowest-priced bid that meets all the requirements, or bids are scored on quality as well as price. Put the criteria and their weights in the bidding document.

Example “Price 70%, service response time 20%, training plan 10%.” (Illustrative weights.)

Ask yourself Could a bidder work out, from the document alone, how the winner will be chosen?

3.4 Give bidders enough time and clear answers

Three things help good suppliers prepare a correct bid. Hold a pre-bid meeting. Share written answers with all bidders. Issue a formal amendment whenever the document changes. After the last amendment, leave enough time for bidders to respond to it.

Example If an amendment changes a technical requirement two days before the deadline, move the deadline.

Ask yourself After your last amendment, how many working days do bidders have left?

3.5 Learn from bidders' questions

Questions from bidders show you where the document is weak. When several bidders ask about the same requirement, it is unclear, or narrower than you meant. Correct it before bids close, not after they are opened.

Example If three bidders ask whether the warmer must have a built-in scale, the requirement is unclear or too narrow.

Ask yourself Which requirement got the most questions, and what did you change because of it?

3.6 Plan what happens after the contract is signed

Signing the contract is the halfway point, not the end. Plan delivery, installation, acceptance testing, user training and the warranty in advance. Decide who will maintain the device after the warranty ends.

Example Write down who keeps the service record, who calls the supplier when a warmer breaks down, and who budgets for spare parts after the warranty.

Ask yourself Who is responsible for this device in the year after the warranty ends?

3.7 If a tender fails, find the cause before you repeat it

Too few bids, no bids that meet the requirements, and prices above the cost estimate (HPS) are three different failures with different causes. If you repeat the same tender without finding the cause, you will usually get the same result.

Example No bid met the requirements because none had a test report for the particular standard. Before re-tendering, check that suppliers can provide one.

Ask yourself Before you re-tender: what exactly failed, and which part of the document have you changed?

Who does what in a government tender

The usual roles under Presidential Regulation 16 of 2018 on government procurement, as amended. Your organisation may divide the work differently. Purchases from the e-catalogue are usually handled by the PPK or a procurement official (Pejabat Pengadaan), not by a Pokja.

RoleUsual tasksMost useful guides
Clinical users (doctors, midwives, nurses, laboratory staff)Describe the clinical need, and check that the specification makes sense in practiceSpecification
Technical and maintenance staff (for example IPSRS in hospitals)Check technical requirements, installation needs, maintenance and spare partsSpecification and Technology
PPKSets the technical specification and the cost estimate (HPS), signs and manages the contract, and receives the goods at handoverSpecification, Pricing and Regulation
Pokja PemilihanPrepares the bidding document, runs the tender, answers bidders' questions and evaluates the bidsProcess and Regulation
Supplier (penyedia)Submits a bid, then delivers, installs, trains and provides service under the contract 

When a tender fails

Three common ways a tender fails, and the causes to check first. More than one can apply.

What happenedCommon causesCheck first
Too few bids, or noneThe lot is too large, the timetable too short, or suppliers did not hear about it.Lot size compared with what suppliers can deliver, the number of days allowed, and where the notice was published.
Bids arrived, but none met the requirementsA requirement is unclear, or only one product can meet it.The bidders' questions, and which requirement each bid missed.
Prices above the cost estimate (HPS)The estimate left out delivery, installation, service or other costs that bidders included.What the estimate covered, compared with what the document asked bidders to include.
