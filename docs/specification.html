What should we ask for?

A specification decides what arrives at the facility. A good specification lets several manufacturers compete to supply a device that meets the clinical need. A poor one lets in equipment that does not work, or leaves only one supplier able to bid.

  • Last reviewed 11 October 2026
  • 7 rules
  • About 6 min to read
  • Checklist for this guide

The 7 rules in this guide

  1. 1.1 Start from the clinical need
  2. 1.2 Describe what it must do, not which product to buy
  3. 1.3 Separate “must have” from “nice to have”
  4. 1.4 Name the right standards
  5. 1.5 Choose for the place where it will be used
  6. 1.6 List everything that must come with the device
  7. 1.7 Make every requirement checkable

1.1 Start from the clinical need

Before you list any features, write down what the device must do, for which patients, and in which type of facility. A device for a district hospital ward and one for a village health post may have the same name but need very different specifications.

Example For an infant warmer: “Keeps a newborn warm during resuscitation and the first hours after birth, in the delivery room of a PONED puskesmas.”

Ask yourself Can a clinician who will use the device explain, in one sentence, why each requirement is needed?

1.2 Describe what it must do, not which product to buy

State the range, accuracy, capacity or output you need. Do not name a brand or model. Do not copy a design detail that only one manufacturer uses. A requirement copied from one brochure usually fits only one product.

Example Write “heater output controlled automatically by a skin temperature sensor, with a manual mode”, not the model name of the warmer you used before.

Ask yourself Can more than one manufacturer meet this requirement as written?

1.3 Separate “must have” from “nice to have”

A “must have” is something without which the device is unsafe or useless, because a bid that lacks it is rejected. Everything else is “nice to have”. If the evaluation method allows it, give extra points for “nice to have” items instead of rejecting bids without them.

Example For an infant warmer, an alarm when the temperature goes out of range is a must have. A built-in baby scale is nice to have.

Ask yourself If a bid did not meet this line, would you really want to reject it?

1.4 Name the right standards

Medical electrical equipment must meet the general safety standard IEC 60601-1. Many types of device also have a particular standard of their own. For example, IEC 60601-2-21 covers infant radiant warmers. Name the standards that apply, and ask for proof such as a test report, not just a statement that the device complies.

Example Weak: “Meets international standards.” Strong: “Meets IEC 60601-1 and IEC 60601-2-21. Attach the test report.”

Ask yourself Does each standard you name actually apply to this type of device?

1.5 Choose for the place where it will be used

The device must keep working where it is used. Think about the electricity supply and the climate: heat, humidity and dust. Think also about the journey to remote facilities, the skills of the staff who will use it, and who will maintain it.

Example A laboratory analyzer for a puskesmas with frequent power cuts may need a UPS, and reagents that can be stored without a refrigerator.

Ask yourself Would this device still work in the facility on the delivery list with the fewest resources?

1.6 List everything that must come with the device

A device is not ready to use without its accessories, its first supply of consumables and a manual its users can read. Include these, and also training, the warranty period, spare parts, and how fast the supplier must respond to a breakdown.

Example For a laboratory analyzer: the first reagents, control and calibration materials, printer paper, and training for the laboratory staff.

Ask yourself On delivery day, will everything needed to treat the first patient be there?

1.7 Make every requirement checkable

For each requirement, say what evidence the bidder must submit, and how you will check it when the device is delivered. If you cannot check a requirement, you cannot enforce it.

Example Requirement: temperature control within the range you set. Evidence in the bid: the test report. Check at delivery: measure with a calibrated thermometer during the acceptance test.

Ask yourself For each line: which document proves it, and which check confirms it at delivery?

Rewriting a requirement

Four lines that often appear in specifications, and how to write each one better. The examples are illustrative.

Often writtenThe problemBetter
“Brand X or equivalent”Names a product, and does not say what “equivalent” means.State what the device must achieve, with the range and accuracy needed.
“High-quality display”Cannot be checked, so it cannot be enforced.“Display shows the set value and the measured value, readable from one metre.”
“Must have a touchscreen”A design choice, not a clinical need.“Controls can be used with gloved hands and cleaned with standard disinfectant.”
“Complies with international standards”Does not say which standards, or what proof is needed.“Meets IEC 60601-1 and the particular standard for this type of device. Test report attached.”

A full specification, section by section

The World Health Organization publishes a template for technical specifications with fifty fields in thirteen sections. If you use the same sections every time, nothing is forgotten and two specifications can be compared line by line.

SectionWhat to write
Name, category and codingThe generic name and a code from a recognised naming system, such as GMDN, so that every bidder understands the same device
Purpose of useThe clinical purpose, the level of care and the department where it will be used
Technical characteristicsWhat it must achieve, what it displays, and the settings a user can change
Physical characteristicsParts, mobility and materials
Utility requirementsThe electricity, water or gas supply the device needs
Accessories, consumables and spare partsWhat must arrive with the device, and what it will use up
PackagingSterility on delivery, shelf life, transport, storage and labelling
Environmental requirementsThe conditions the device must tolerate where it will work
Training, installation and useSite preparation, approval at commissioning, and training for users
Warranty and maintenanceWarranty period, type of service contract, spare parts after the warranty, and upgrades
DocumentationThe manuals and other documents to be supplied
DecommissioningHow long the device is expected to last
Safety and standardsRisk class, marketing authorisation and the standards that apply
