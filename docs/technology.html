How do we judge the technology?

You do not need to design a device to judge it. You need to know how it works, what evidence supports it, and whether it suits the place where it will be used.

  • Last reviewed 11 October 2026
  • 7 rules
  • About 5 min to read
  • Checklist for this guide

The 7 rules in this guide

  1. B.1 Understand how it works
  2. B.2 Separate what matters for the patient from extra features
  3. B.3 Ask what evidence supports each claim
  4. B.4 Check its history in use
  5. B.5 Match the technology to the level of care
  6. B.6 Plan for the device's whole life
  7. B.7 Be careful with new technology

B.1 Understand how it works

Every device measures or delivers something physical, such as heat, pressure, light or an electrical signal. Knowing which one tells you what limits its accuracy, what it needs from its surroundings, and what usually breaks first.

Example A radiant warmer heats the baby from above with infrared heat, so staff can care for the baby without covering it. That is why it needs a skin temperature sensor to control the heat.

Ask yourself Can you explain in two sentences how this device does its job?

B.2 Separate what matters for the patient from extra features

The core technology decides how well the device works for the patient. Extra features add convenience, and often add cost. Two devices with the same core technology can have very different prices because of screens, connectivity or software that change nothing for the patient.

Example For an analyzer, the measuring method decides how accurate the result is. A larger touchscreen or Wi-Fi does not.

Ask yourself Which of the listed features changes the result for the patient?

B.3 Ask what evidence supports each claim

Some evidence is stronger than other evidence. An independent test report or a peer-reviewed study is stronger than the manufacturer's own data, and both are stronger than a brochure. A marketing authorisation shows that a device may be sold. It does not show that it is the right choice for you.

Example A brochure says “highly accurate”. An independent evaluation reports how close the results were to a reference method, and on how many samples.

Ask yourself For the main performance claim: who measured it, and were they independent of the seller?

B.4 Check its history in use

Find out how long the model has been sold, where it is used, and whether it has been recalled or named in a safety notice. Facilities that already use it are the best source of information, and they are usually willing to talk.

Example Ask the seller for three facilities that have used the model for more than a year, and call at least one of them.

Ask yourself Where is this model already in use, and what do those users say about it?

B.5 Match the technology to the level of care

The most advanced device is not the best choice for every facility. WHO, UNFPA and UNICEF publish a joint list of priority medical devices for the health of mothers, newborns and children. It is arranged by level of care. WHO's MeDevIS platform lists more than 2,300 types of device and the level of care each one suits. In Indonesia, a health technology assessment committee, set up by the Ministry of Health in 2014, reviews the evidence for technologies considered for JKN coverage.

Example A fully automatic chemistry analyzer may suit a district hospital laboratory. A puskesmas may be better served by a smaller semi-automatic analyzer that its own staff can run and maintain.

Ask yourself Was this technology designed for a facility like yours, with your staff and your number of patients?

B.6 Plan for the device's whole life

A device is used for many years after its warranty ends. Ask how long spare parts will be available. Ask how the software is updated, and who pays for updates. Ask when the manufacturer plans to stop supporting the model.

Example Ask the bidder to state in writing until which year spare parts for this model will be available.

Ask yourself For how many years does the manufacturer promise spare parts and software support?

B.7 Be careful with new technology

New technology can reach the market before there is evidence that it works well. If you buy early, you pay to discover its problems. For anything new, look for evaluations by people other than the manufacturer before you buy it for a whole district.

Example Try a new rapid test in one facility, and compare its results with the laboratory, before you buy it for every puskesmas.

Ask yourself Has this technology been evaluated by anyone who does not profit from selling it?
