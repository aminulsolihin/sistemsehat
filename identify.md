rules:
  - number: "C.1"
    heading: Find needs by watching care
    explanation: >-
      Start where care is given, not with a technology. Watch procedures, interview patients, staff
      and managers, and note every problem, delay and workaround you see. Surveys can then show how
      common a problem is.
    example: >-
      Spend a week in a puskesmas delivery room and with its referral ambulance. Write down every
      delay, improvised fix and complaint, before you think of any device.
    ask: How many needs have you written down, and how many came from watching care yourself?

  - number: "C.2"
    heading: Write each need as problem, population and outcome
    explanation: >-
      Biodesign's form is "a way to address a problem, in a population, that achieves an outcome".
      Make the outcome measurable, such as fewer complications or a shorter stay. Leave out any
      solution, so that very different ideas can still compete.
    example: >-
      Weak: "A cheaper incubator." Better: "A way to prevent hypothermia in low-birth-weight babies
      during transfer from a puskesmas to the district hospital."
    ask: Does your need statement name a problem, a population and an outcome, and no technology?

  - number: "C.3"
    heading: Research each need before you choose one
    explanation: >-
      For each need, study the disease, the solutions that exist, the people involved and who pays,
      and the size of the market. Then compare your needs on what you learned. Choose the one that
      matters most and that your team can act on.
    example: >-
      A team compares three needs on the number of patients affected, the solutions that already
      exist, and whether a puskesmas could afford something new. It chooses the one with the
      largest unmet gap.
    ask: Why did you choose this need over the others on your list?

  - number: "C.4"
    heading: Turn your research into need criteria
    explanation: >-
      Need criteria list what any solution must do, based on your observations and interviews, not
      on an idea you already like. In low-resource settings they often include low cost, local
      manufacture, tolerance of dust and humidity, an irregular power supply, little training, and
      easy repair. A WHO target product profile sets the same kind of criteria as minimum and preferred
      values.
    example: >-
      Must have: keeps the baby's skin temperature in the safe range for two hours without mains
      power. Nice to have: one person can carry it. (Illustrative figures.)
    ask: Can each must-have criterion be traced to something you observed or read?

  - number: "C.5"
    heading: Generate many concepts, then screen them
    explanation: >-
      Brainstorm with clinicians, engineers and users together: defer judgement, build on each
      other's ideas, and aim for quantity. Then score each concept against the need criteria. Drop
      any concept that fails a must-have criterion.
    example: >-
      A midwife, an electrical engineer and a public health student list 40 ways to keep babies warm
      in transfer. Screening against the criteria leaves three.
    ask: Which criterion ruled out each concept you dropped?

  - number: "C.6"
    heading: Build each prototype to answer one question
    explanation: >-
      Decide what you need to learn before you build. A works-like model tests the function, a
      looks-like model tests size and handling, and an is-like model comes close to the final device.
      The more questions one prototype tries to answer, the harder it is to build and to learn from.
    example: >-
      A cardboard looks-like model answers whether the warmer fits in the ambulance. A works-like
      heater on a bench answers how long the battery lasts. Neither needs to be the final device.
    ask: What question is your next prototype meant to answer?

  - number: "C.7"
    heading: Check patents, regulation and payment early
    explanation: >-
      Before you choose a final concept, check who holds patents in the area and how it would be
      regulated. Check who would pay for it, and what business model would sustain it. Any of these
      can stop a good idea late. [Guide E](to-market.html) covers the route to market in Indonesia.
    example: >-
      A concept that is only affordable with a new JKN tariff may take years longer than one that a
      hospital can buy outright.
    ask: Who will pay for your solution, and from which budget?

## Go deeper with Stanford Biodesign

Each rule here condenses a stage of the Stanford Biodesign process. The textbook covers it in full, with case studies and a Global Perspectives section that covers Africa, India and other regions. The online Biodesign Guide adds worked examples and videos; its chapters open after free registration.

## Start with a team: health hackathons

MIT Hacking Medicine has run health hackathons since 2011. Clinicians pitch problems, not products, and mixed teams of engineers, clinicians and business students work on them over a weekend. Its principles are to start from the problem, mix disciplines, and change course quickly when an idea does not work.

Hackathons are good at forming teams and testing needs fast. A survey of 12 hackathons in India, Uganda and the United States found that 30.3% of projects had made progress about a year later. Treat a hackathon as a start: it does not replace the research in rules C.1 to C.4.
